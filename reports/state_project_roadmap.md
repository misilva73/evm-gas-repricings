# State Project Roadmap

### Carlos and Maria, July 2026

## Goals and direction

Two changes are making us rethink how Ethereum handles state.

- **zkevm.** Once proofs are mandatory in-protocol, every block ships with a proof by default. Attesting and fully-validating nodes then verify that proof and sample data via PeerDAS instead of re-executing. They no longer need to hold state, so far fewer nodes have a reason to.
- **State growth.** As we scale toward gigagas, holding and serving the full state gets harder. State grows about 100 GB/yr today. Even a conservative 20x target implies roughly 2 TB/yr, or about 8 TB after four years. Pricing and new state types help. But pricing alone cannot hold growth flat while also scaling throughput. So the work of holding, serving, and syncing has to keep pace.

**Target.** Support a roughly 20x gas-limit increase, toward gigagas. Keep it feasible to build, serve, and partially-validate state on home-staker and home-builder hardware. Preserve a decentralized, non-custodial path to head-state access.

### What we want out of state

These are the properties we hold every workstream accountable to. Each is phrased so a workstream can *fail* it, rather than as a slogan.

- **Small and fast proofs, per state tier.** Today's MPT proofs are far too big and costly, both in bandwidth and compute. The base tier's proofs must get much smaller. They must also become attractive enough to actually be *used* (see verified serving below). At the same time, some tiers may deliberately give up proof-optimality to gain storage density or access speed instead. Proof performance is a per-tier design choice, not a global one.
- **Cheap to store.** Avoid forcing hardware upgrades on node operators. Give big, slow, cheap disks (HDDs) a job. The cold tier should tolerate them.
- **Geographically distributed.** We hold this against three threat models. No single jurisdiction can cut off or seize state access. No correlated datacenter or provider failure (Hetzner, OVH, AWS) takes out most holders. And users worldwide have a nearby server for fast reads.
- **Syncing feasible long-term.** Sync cost must never gate joining the network, whether as a builder, includer, or server.
- **Throughput.** The roughly 20x target above.
- **Many types of state, not one.** We treat heterogeneity as a design principle. Each tier picks its own point on the (proof size, proof speed, storage cost, update cost) frontier, rather than one structure serving every need badly.
- **Held by as many machines, and served by as many entities, as possible.** This is the north star, made concrete. It means permissionless entry at low hardware cost, a minimum level of independent replication for every state slice, defaults that keep holding-and-serving the common behavior, and a way to measure all of the above (see "Validate demand and measure the base" below). The EF's [Future of Ethereum's State](https://blog.ethereum.org/en/2025/12/16/future-of-state) warns that state otherwise concentrates with builders, RPC providers, and specialist operators. This goal is the counterweight.

### Who still needs state after mandatory proofs

The set of state-holders shrinks to those who structurally require it.

- **Builders** (including local "home" builders running vanilla software). They need the full state to build any block.
- **FOCIL includers.** They need the **VOPS** state ([Validity-Only Partial Statelessness](https://ethresear.ch/t/a-pragmatic-path-towards-validity-only-partial-statelessness-vops/22236)) to produce inclusion lists. This is much smaller. It sits under 1 TB even with no changes, plausibly under 512 or 256 GB, and can be optimized further.
- **Users / RPC.** Anyone executing against head state. Today this requires the whole DB, which is why users fall back to centralized providers.

**Who we are building for.** In practice the work targets the **~10k reachable nodes** on the network today (per [Etherscan](https://etherscan.io/nodetracker), [ethernodes](https://ethernodes.org/), [MigaLabs/monitoreth](https://monitoreth.io/nodes), and the [500k-validator milestone](https://blockworks.co/news/ethereum-to-reach-500000-validators)). Within that group, home stakers are the core target. They are the operators most aligned with where we want things to go. Most run validators out of interest or altruism rather than profit. Solo staking pays full rewards with no pool fee. But it carries far higher reward variance ([CF Benchmarks](https://blog.cfbenchmarks.com/content/files/2024/10/Ethereum-Staking-Reward-Dynamics---CF-Benchmarks.pdf) show variance falls exponentially as an operator's validator count grows), plus the burden of running a machine. So pooling via Lido is the easier economic choice. This shapes the incentives discussion below.

### What we want to avoid

- Users having no alternative to centralized providers (Infura, Alchemy, and the like) for verified, trustless state access.
- New builders barred from joining because they cannot sync or store the full state.
- FOCIL includers barred from joining because they cannot sync the VOPS state.

## How we work

- **De-risk open questions first.** Where a decision hinges on something we don't yet know, we resolve the uncertainty before committing. We assess the benefit before building (hot/cold separation), confirm necessity before speccing (binary tree), and benchmark before pricing (EIP-8037-style). The point is to avoid sinking effort into work that a later answer could invalidate.
- **Validate demand and measure the base.** This is the same de-risking discipline applied to the demand side. Several load-bearing assumptions are currently unmeasured. We don't know whether node operators actually want to serve state, whether apps would adopt new state tiers, what hardware the ~10k reachable nodes actually run, or why light clients haven't taken off. So before investing, we check. Operator hardware surveys, consultations with the top state-creating apps, and user-demand checks are part of the plan, not afterthoughts.
- **Ship iteratively.** We prefer near-term, net-positive steps that stand on their own over big-bang changes. For example, the centralized "Nginx-like" aggregator comes before a fully distributed state network, and the state tiering pays off independently of the trie migration.
- **Parallel, loosely-coupled workstreams.** The five near-term tracks proceed largely in parallel and unconditionally. We keep dependencies explicit so they can move independently until the points where they genuinely must converge.
- **Open collaboration.** The project is large, the team is small, and the changes span the whole stack. So this has to be an ecosystem effort. We lean on collaborations with aligned teams and individuals, and focus our own effort on the high-leverage tasks and on coordination, rather than trying to build everything ourselves.
- **Keep exploration always on.** We want a recurring window to stay current with ecosystem improvements and run tightly-scoped exploration. Keeping up this research is necessary to do our work in the most informed way. The trick is to protect it without letting it delay our priority work for each fork.

## What do we need to solve?

This section breaks the state-related work into six workstreams. The first five share one umbrella goal: keep building, serving, and partially-validating state feasible on home-staker and home-builder hardware, even under gigagas throughput. The sixth ships a trie built for a post-zkevm, gigagas chain.

This is an extensive list of everything we *could* do. After it, we discuss priorities, sequencing, and what work can be deferred.

### 1. State serving / RPC decentralization

Goal: give users a decentralized path to *retrieve* head state without defaulting to centralized RPC, and keep syncing that state permissionless.

- **State serving workflow.** Today any full node can already serve everything a normal user needs: balances, call simulation, gas estimation, and proofs. Only archive is missing, and almost no one needs that. The real gap is **discovery**. Users don't know which node to ask, so they fall back to Infura or Alchemy. We frame this as a **"state network" workstream** with three phases.
  - *Near-term first phase.* A more centralized **"Nginx-like" aggregator and load-balancer** that existing full nodes subscribe to, distributing user requests across peers. This lets the thousands of nodes already holding state (and doing nothing with it beyond SnapSync) actually serve users. It is very likely a no-brainer. Still, we treat it as a hypothesis until operator outreach confirms nodes will opt in (see "Validate demand and measure the base"). The phase-1 design must also consider read privacy. A user's queries expose their address-level interest to whichever operator serves them, so IP stripping and query mixing belong in the aggregator spec (see Risks). Likely pursued via a DappNode collaboration or grant.
  - *Middle phase.* State sharded among home nodes, with the **aggregator doing the routing**. Each serving node holds a slice plus the account trie (see partial-stateful serving below), so the group covers the full state without any single machine holding it all.
  - *End-game.* A truly distributed state network (DHT or IPFS-like). This is far enough out, and underspecified enough, that it should **not** drive current design decisions. The [Portal Network](https://ethportal.net/resources/faq) is the cautionary tale. Its state network never left early development and [trin is no longer maintained](https://github.com/ethereum/trin). That is evidence for the centralized-first phasing, not against the end goal.

  The infrastructure (sharded state, small-enough proofs, latency, the user-facing API) is the same whether serving is altruistic or incentivized. So this work proceeds independently of the incentives question. Note that serving state to users covers the RPC calls needed to submit transactions. It is distinct from being a full public RPC, which also implies profit-seeking and broad discoverability.
- **Verified serving: proofs as first-class products.** Every state response should be able to carry a proof, verifiable against a head state root that mandatory proofs make universally trustworthy. **SNARK-wrapping MPT branches gives roughly constant-size query proofs without any tree change.** Generation cost and latency are the things to benchmark. This is also the moat versus centralized RPC: serving proofs at scale is what centralized providers can't do cheaply (see incentives below). And it has anchor customers today. **L2s and bridges are the biggest real consumers of state proofs**, and the loudest breakage victims of any commitment change, so they belong in this workstream's design loop. 🤔 Related research: **proof-carried trie updates**, meaning proofs or BAL extensions that ship the updated intermediate nodes along touched paths. Trie-holding nodes then skip re-hashing, and flat-state nodes stay current entirely from proven diffs (ZKEVM or MPT).
- **Partial-stateful serving nodes.** A node can hold the full account trie (~60 GB, enough to keep *generating* account and hot-state proofs) while shedding the multi-TB contract-storage long tail, and still serve most user needs. This lowers the hardware bar to join the serving set. It directly serves the "served by as many entities as possible" goal.
- **Onboarding new home-nodes** 🤔. Beyond defending the existing ~10k, can we grow the set? Candidate value propositions are self-sovereign RPC, read privacy, and staking. This is a hypothesis to test via outreach before we invest.
- **Archive-node satellites as a backstop.** A handful of well-known heavy or archive operators, pinned as sync-bootstrap seeders of last resort, so syncing never dies even if regular serving decays. Archive and history serving also needs an owner for the resurrection path of expiring state tiers (era files or torrents, see new state types).
- **Syncing.** Being a builder must stay permissionless and not unreasonable to set up. Even at perfect efficiency, syncing multi-TB state is slow and can hit bandwidth caps. We plan to use BAL-assisted, executionless sync plus P2P improvements. The artifacts now exist: [EIP-7928](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-7928) block-level access lists (a Glamsterdam headliner), [EIP-8159](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-8159) (eth/71 BAL exchange), and [EIP-8189](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-8189) (snap/2, BAL-based state healing). 🤔 We should also set a quantified sync budget (for example, a fresh sync in at most N days on home bandwidth at target state size) so "viable" stays measurable.

### 2. State tiering

Goal: client DBs that stay cheap to operate at multi-TB state.

Today's client DBs weren't built for multi-TB state. Per-write cost grows as $O(\log^2 n)$, and blows up once state outgrows RAM. The fix is **hot/cold separation**. We keep hot state in the live DB and push cold state to flat files on cheaper media. This lowers operational cost, raises throughput, and brings down the cost of running a node.

The hypothesis is now backed by data. [EIP-8188](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-8188) specifies last-written-block hot/cold metadata (no gas changes). Measurements show that about 94% of storage writes hit state from the last 30 days, and that the top 1% of accounts absorb 96–98% of reads ([Hot-Cold Storage Separation in Practice](https://ethresear.ch/t/hot-cold-storage-separation-in-practice/25119), [The Anatomy of Ethereum's State Access](https://ethresear.ch/t/the-anatomy-of-ethereum-s-state-access/25317)). Cold state should target **HDDs**, since big, slow, cheap disks need a job.

Hot/cold separation also has a pricing side. [EIP-8038](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-8038) raises cold-access costs, and [EIP-8057](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-8057) discounts recently-touched state (accesses within the last 32 blocks, tracked via BALs). 🤔 **Witness-carrying transactions** could also earn a discount for shipping witnesses of the cold state they touch. We keep the assess-first stance, with one caveat. This makes gas depend on recent-block access history, so pricing becomes stateful. BALs make it trackable, but the edge cases get subtler.

Two parallel post-mandatory-proofs directions to explore.

- **Flat-state-only clients** 🤔. Once every block carries a validity proof and a [BAL](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-7928), a node can hold flat KV state only, with no trie at all, staying current by applying proven BAL diffs. This is the cheapest possible state-holding node (intermediate trie nodes are roughly half the DB) and a big win for "held by as many machines as possible." The honest caveat is that such nodes cannot generate Merkle proofs or serve snap-sync range proofs. So trie-keeping nodes (partial-stateful servers, archive satellites) remain necessary. The tiers complement each other rather than one replacing the other.
- **Lthash flat state roots** 🤔. Tempo's [TIP-1078](https://tips.sh/1078) replaces the MPT state root with a flat homomorphic accumulator (BLAKE3-based lthash16). It keeps one lattice element per account or slot, and root updates are just subtract-old plus add-new. There are no intermediate nodes, no re-hashing, and order doesn't matter. It has **no membership proofs**, which is exactly why it only becomes viable under mandatory proofs, where proof-chain induction replaces per-access authentication. If it works, it removes intermediate-node storage, most of the state share of in-guest proving cost, *and* the tree-migration problem (a background shadow pass builds the accumulator, with no structural conversion). The costs are real. It adds a new consensus-critical cryptographic assumption that needs dedicated cryptanalysis, and it breaks every MPT-proof consumer (bridges, L2s), so SNARK query proofs (see verified serving) must exist first. Worth exploring, but not a committed direction.

### 3. State-growth control (pricing + new state types)

Goal: keep the growth rate controllable while throughput scales, without expiring existing state.

- **State pricing.** [EIP-7999](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-7999) for the unified fee market, and cap the growth rate on dynamic state. Review state clearing pricing.
- **New types of state** (instead of state expiry, see the note below). Introduce cheaper, more restrictive tiers alongside existing state. These are **temporary storage** (a tree zeroed each period, for example monthly) and **UTXOs**, with out-of-order **resurrection** via per-period spent/unspent bitfields. Permanent storage stays for accounts, code, and core composable contracts. Balances, NFTs, and short-lived event state move to the cheaper tiers. This is opt-in, app-by-app. See [Hyper-scaling state by creating new forms of state](https://ethresear.ch/t/hyper-scaling-state-by-creating-new-forms-of-state/24052).
- **Write-once state** 🤔. An opt-in tier for state written once and never mutated. No proposal exists under this exact name. The closest relative is the UTXO variant in [Hyper-scaling state](https://ethresear.ch/t/hyper-scaling-state-by-creating-new-forms-of-state/24052). The properties compound. It is never re-hashed on update, it's friendly to append-only accumulators, and it's ideal for cold or HDD media. On top of that, **proofs of write-once state never invalidate**, so they can be cached, replicated, and CDN'd indefinitely. Serving this tier becomes trivial. The open design questions are enforcement (tier declaration at creation) and pricing (pay-once-store-forever must be priced honestly). Crucially, this is **opt-in for new state only**. It is never a forced conversion of existing state, which would be expiry by another name (see the note below).
- **Privacy-tailored state.** Privacy protocols are the strongest concrete customer for the new tiers. Commitment trees are append-only, and nullifier sets are write-once *by construction*. Designing for them turns privacy from a hypothetical trigger into a named consumer. The honest note is that nullifier sets grow forever by design. So the tier they live in must meter and structurally handle unbounded growth (accumulators, epoching).
- **Multi-roots in the block** 🤔. This is the enabling mechanism for all of the above. The header commits to one root per state tier, and each tier picks its own commitment scheme, that is, its own point on the (proof size, proof speed, storage cost, update cost) frontier. No standalone proposal exists. The idea appears embedded in overlay-migration designs ([EIP-7612](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-7612)) and implicitly in [Hyper-scaling state](https://ethresear.ch/t/hyper-scaling-state-by-creating-new-forms-of-state/24052). The costs to spec honestly are header and engine-API changes, cross-tier transaction atomicity, and per-tier tooling and wallet complexity. (Vitalik's [2018 multi-root analysis](https://ethresear.ch/t/detailed-analysis-of-stateless-client-witness-size-and-gains-from-batching-and-multi-state-roots/862) found negligible *witness* savings, but that targeted a different goal and doesn't refute the storage-model use.)

### 4. State-serving incentives

Goal: a sustainable answer to *why* nodes hold and serve state.

There are two main paths. The first is to **mandate holding**: make holding and serving a small slice of state (say ~300 GB, not the whole thing) a condition for validator rewards, leaning on the power of defaults. The second is to **make serving profitable**: change the protocol so serving state earns a small recurring revenue, for example a market for ZK state proofs, which centralized providers can't serve at scale because proofs are expensive to generate. The second path is harder but the only long-term-sustainable one. The target audience is the ~10,000–11,000 reachable nodes running today, with home stakers as the core subset (see "Who we are building for" above).

There is also a pragmatic third path that needs no protocol change and can start now: **out-of-protocol revenue sharing via the aggregator**. This means premium access (API keys, SLAs) with revenue kicked back to the home nodes serving behind it. [Lava Network](https://www.lavanet.xyz/) shows out-of-protocol RPC markets work at scale. It doubles as a live experiment feeding the long-term research. One requirement is non-negotiable from day one: **sybil resistance**. The moment serving pays, fake serving follows (self-dealt queries, sybil backends). As the EF's [Future of Ethereum's State](https://blog.ethereum.org/en/2025/12/16/future-of-state) puts it, "there's currently no good way to prove entities actually serve state." Note also that the power of defaults, which the first path leans on, is one of the operational mechanisms under "What we want out of state" above.

### 5. Partial statelessness / VOPS

Goal: FOCIL includers operate on a small validity-only subset of state instead of the full set.

- **Prototype a VOPS client.** Build or extend a client that runs on the validity-only subset, shedding the state a node does not need to produce inclusion lists and validate. Partial-stateful implementations are already proven and documented, and full VOPS support in Geth is about a month of work.
- **Keep VOPS nodes current and syncable.** After BALs and mandatory proofs, partial nodes keep their slice current by applying **proven BAL diffs**. No Merkle witnesses sit on their critical path (witnesses matter mainly for snap-sync range proofs and serving). So the binary tree is *not* a prerequisite here (see the binary-tree section). We still need syncing from other VOPS nodes to stay viable, and the builder-syncing solutions apply to includers too.
- **Keep account abstraction scoped.** AA stores extra data per account on top of the account tree. If it grows unchecked, it inflates VOPS. A partial-stateless node can hold the account tree plus the first N storage slots of each account's subtree, which is viable only if N stays small (2–3). So we need to keep AA scoped.

### 6. Binary tree — new trie structure (design + migration)

Goal: a trie built for small proofs and proving-friendliness on a post-zkevm, gigagas chain. Migration is part of this workstream, so design and rollout stay together.

- **Small proofs through a binary tree.** There are two options: a full tree migration ([EIP-7864](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-7864)), or a [partial binary tree](https://cperezz.github.io/pbt-spec/) where we move everything except contract storage. We decide on the final design and prototype, but only once a re-entry trigger fires (see below). Note that even a partial transition (accounts plus code chunking, leaving the storage tree on MPT) will break a meaningful part of the ecosystem. CowSwap-style contracts, for instance, would need upgrades.
- **zk-friendly hash.** BLAKE3 as the interim hash, and Poseidon2 as the target, pending the dedicated Poseidon cryptanalysis effort. Tree finalization is gated on this go/no-go.
- **Code chunking.** Code lives in the tree, chunked so only touched chunks need witnesses. This needs updated pricing, and we can likely bump the contract size limit.
- **Friendly for heavy witness consumers.** Witnesses small enough that serving and sync-range proofs stay practical at scale. (Note that VOPS itself no longer depends on this. After BALs, partial nodes update from proven diffs. See partial statelessness.)
- **Migration.** MPT to binary tree is a one-time conversion over the full live state. This is historically the part that sinks timelines. We need to decide overlay-tree versus flag-day conversion, who bears the conversion cost, and how long clients run dual trees. The witness format must agree with the zkEVM execution-witness and stateless-guest interface.

> **Validating viability with the ecosystem.** Alongside the trigger question, we want to actively surface any feedback that would make us *not* do the migration at all, for example a major app or wallet (Uniswap, MetaMask) for whom this is unworkable. We treat "things will break" not as a veto but as a signal of how much warning the ecosystem needs to adapt. A genuine blocker would be something stronger. We will consult client teams and major app, wallet, and contract owners, and use the answers to confirm or reconsider the project.

## Priorities and sequencing

**Priorities.** If forced to rank, the order is:

1. **State serving / RPC-decentralization.** Raising growth targets and scaling throughput deepens centralization unless a decentralized retrieval path exists first. This is the highest priority and the most under-explored. The infrastructure for serving state (tx-submission APIs, latency, small-enough proofs) is the same whether or not serving is incentivized, so this track proceeds regardless of how the incentives question resolves. It is also independent of ACD, so it can proceed in parallel with the other workstreams.

   Note: Being able to *spin up new RPCs* is not the same thing, and it does not on its own preserve permissionless, censorship-resistant access. Two failure modes show why. **(1) Resilience failure**: an outage or a major government action takes down the large centralized providers. The network is still live, but any user not running a full node with full state can no longer submit transactions or use Ethereum, and new providers take time to materialize, so the outage persists for as long as that takes. **(2) Censorship failure**: the large providers keep working but start censoring certain users or parts of the state. Some users transact normally while others cannot, and new providers are not guaranteed to serve the censored set, especially when it is small enough to be unprofitable. In both cases, "someone will spin up an RPC" is not a real safeguard. The only durable protection is a diverse set of nodes holding and serving state across jurisdictions and hardware setups, independent of any centralized provider. Building that set is exactly what this workstream does, which is why it ranks first.
2. **Binary tree + zk-friendly hash.** Code chunking and the trie hash function are blockers for mandatory proofs with gigagas blocks, so we need to ship this before M*. On top of that, a one-time MPT to binary-tree conversion scales with the size of the live state. The longer we wait, the larger and slower the conversion, and some approaches feasible today may become infeasible later. The migration is a massive amount of work, complexity, and risk. We need to decide on the design and start prototyping with clients before the end of the year.
3. **State-growth control.** Near-term, net-positive work that is largely independent of the trie migration. Multidimensional pricing controls the growth rate. New types of state let us scale key use cases (such as privacy) and make them cheaper to end users. This is lower risk and largely additive.

**What we are deferring.** The following points will not be a short-term focus. We can still do short exploration and research on them to keep up with the ecosystem and stay ready to pivot if needed.

- **Partial statelessness / VOPS.** Today total state is ~293 GB (account tree ~60 GB, VOPS state ~15–18 GB). Partial-stateful implementations are proven and documented, and full VOPS support in Geth is about a month of work. State size is not currently a blocker for a node to run or act as an includer. The real entry barrier is having to run and maintain a machine at all. At today's sizes the CL state (Prysm, ~100–200 GB) is already larger than the account state, so FOCIL includers are not our biggest issue. This assumes we keep the current state growth rate. Before increasing that rate, we need to figure out state serving. And for syncing, which must stay viable, the solutions for builder syncing can be used for includers as well.
- **State tiering.** The real impact lands once we start scaling state growth and state approaches the TB mark, which argues for doing it after — or alongside — mandatory proofs rather than now. Given the volume of work across the other tracks, this is one component we can comfortably defer, keeping only low-priority exploration going.
- **State-serving incentives.** A mid-term, first-principles research question: mandate holding versus make serving profitable. We do not need to solve it now. We have time because growth stays controllable through existing pricing machinery (keeping EIP-8037 in place). That said, shipping the "Nginx-like" aggregator and load-balancer on top of DappNodes directly tests how far we can rely on altruism as the alternative to centralized providers under the two failure modes above. It tells us how feasible and performant such a design is, and how credible it is as a fallback. We expect most users to keep defaulting to centralized RPCs, so the economics are hard, and adding protocol-level incentives for state serving is a large open question with no guaranteed good solution. If aligned, altruistic nodes turn out to be enough, much of that complexity is sorted. So we build the aggregator now and defer the formal incentives design.

**Fork sequencing**. Rough idea of fork items (the I\*/J\* ordering of PBT versus new types of state is under active discussion, per above):

- Multidimensional pricing (EIP-7999) in Hegota
- PBT (partial binary tree) in I\*
- New types of state in J\*
- State tiering in K\*

The ordering proposed places PBT (partial binary tree) ahead of new types of state, matching the priority ranking. But there is a real case for swapping them. PBT is largely a clean-up feature: its main payoff is enabling zkevm and scaling, not a direct user-facing win. New types of state, by contrast, have immediate user impact as they let us scale key use cases (privacy, short-lived data) and make them cheaper for end users. State tiering is similar in kind, letting nodes run cheaper hardware while holding more state.

Reversing the order (new types of state in I\*, PBT in J\*) would ship user-facing value sooner and buy more time to settle the zk-friendly hash function, which PBT depends on. Because mandatory proofs are not scheduled until K\*, PBT still lands in time. The trade-off is the cost of delaying the trie transition: the longer we wait, the larger the live state and the more expensive and risky the one-time migration becomes, albeit at a controlled growth rate. There is also an argument for keeping PBT isolated — it is a massive EL change, and we would rather not pack it into the same fork as another large change like new types of state.
